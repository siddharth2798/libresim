Part GEN · Before you fly

GEN 1Overview

Region Aircraft What both share

libresim is a local program. There is no account and no server. The only network use is optional: a live weather report, and the text-to-speech already on your computer.

The region

  • Detailed area. About 145 × 115 km around Mumbai, Navi Mumbai, Thane creek, the Western Ghats and Pune. Terrain is 30 m, imagery 10 m, with 3D buildings from OpenStreetMap.
  • Outer ring. 90 m terrain and 80 m imagery out to about 245 km: Nashik, Ahmednagar, Satara, Kolhapur and the Konkan coast.
  • Corridor. A coastal strip to Kochi at 30 m terrain and 20 m imagery, with Goa, Mangaluru, Kannur, Kozhikode and Kochi, and 13 more airfields.
  • Beyond. Out to about 490 km the terrain is filled from the nearest real data, so the world has no edge.

The two aircraft

Both are chosen on the start screen and from --aircraft=. Training lessons always use the Cessna.

C172The Skyhawk SP uses the FlightGear c172p model and cockpit, and a flight model checked against the Pilot's Operating Handbook. It has no autopilot. Full numbers are in OPS 2, and one whole flight, start to finish, is in Flying the Cessna.

A320The airliner uses the FlightGear A320-family model and data (GPL-2.0). It is not an Airbus product and it is not affiliated with Airbus. It has a normal law, FADEC, an FCU, a lite MCDU and glass displays. How to fly it, with the autopilot, is in OPS 3 and Flying with the autopilot. What is still simplified is in the limits. The working list is docs/A320-STATUS.md.

What both share

  • The same world: runways and taxiways from OpenStreetMap, stands, follow-me, pushback, ground vehicles, night lighting, and landmark terminals.
  • Runways painted to the ICAO pattern for their width and length: threshold stripes, the numbers, touchdown-zone bars and aiming point. Each one is worn in its own way, with black tyre rubber where airliners land, sealed cracks, patches and faded paint. Your own landings leave fresh tyre marks where your wheels touched. They stay in saved flights and are cleared on a new flight or Restart (F5).
  • Weather from a preset or, if you turn it on, a live METAR plus a forecast. Ridge lift, thermals, storms, rain and icing act on the aircraft.
  • A radio with a controller that follows the flight, checklists, failures, saves, a 10-minute replay, and video recording.
  • Radio navigation: VOR, NDB, DME and the ILS. Sixteen runway ends at ten airports have the real published ILS, and two more airports have a simulated one. Both aircraft can fly it: Flying an ILS approach.
  • Paint. Each aircraft has its own colours and sticker slots. Wings on the airliner stay white.

NEWWhat's new

Flying Sound Routes Airports and runways Graphics Weather Loading and speed Keys and install

This is what changed since the 7 November 2026 handbook, in plain terms. Each line links to the chapter with the detail.

Flying

  • The parking brake holds. A parked aircraft no longer creeps on a slope, at idle or in wind. The Cessna's instrument panel also moves smoothly at lower frame rates.
  • The airliner flies a whole route on the autopilot, to a stop. Plan a route with an arrival runway, take off, engage both autopilots, and the aircraft climbs, cruises, descends, slows down, joins the ILS and lands itself. Several links in that chain were broken and are fixed: the levers-to-CL prompt now always comes after takeoff, a planned route selects managed speed, the managed speed slows to green dot near the destination so the flaps can come out, climbs and descents no longer go the wrong way to fix the speed, the descent starts early enough and ends below the glide slope, and a route arriving from the far side of the airport gets a base fix. The autoland flare is shorter: it no longer floats down the runway losing speed, so the wheels touch about 100 m earlier and the nose is about 1 to 2° lower (it was close to the tail clearance in a head wind). See A flight with the autopilot.
  • A full guide to ILS approaches. A new chapter, Flying an ILS approach, explains the beams, lists every runway with an ILS, shows what each instrument says, how to tune, and an approach step by step in the Cessna and in the airliner, with the autoland and a troubleshooting list.
  • The airliner lands itself on an ILS (autoland). With both autopilots, the gear down and flaps at CONF 3 or FULL, the FMA shows CAT3 DUAL, then LAND below 400 ft, FLARE below 40 ft, thrust to idle and ROLL OUT after touchdown, with the rudder and nose wheel holding the centreline. In the project's tests, in calm air and in winds of up to 20 kt from every side with light and moderate turbulence, it touched down 350 to 560 m past the threshold, within 3 m of the centreline, at up to 200 ft/min. Without the second autopilot or the configuration it lets go at 50 ft as before. The glide slope also now captures from a little above the path (it never captured an aircraft more than 15 m high). Details and what is simplified in Autoland, ILS approach and landing and what the autopilot does not do.
  • The airliner's autopilot has a guide. Flying with the autopilot covers the panel, what each knob does, how to read the mode display (the FMA), a flight step by step, and what is not modelled. Flying the A320 and Flying the Cessna take you from cold start to shutdown.
  • Autopilot disconnect and failure. The airliner's autopilot can be disconnected from the keyboard, a joystick button or a button on the small FCU, with the red AP OFF warning and the tone. A new Autopilot failure (F9, or the start screen's failures step) drops both autopilots and stops them engaging. See the part on disconnecting and the failure list.
  • The wheel turns cockpit knobs. In the 3D cockpit, point at an FCU, EFIS or other knob and turn the mouse wheel: one step per notch, up is more. Elsewhere the wheel zooms as before.
  • The FCU works. Every button and knob on the airliner's autopilot panel (the FCU) and the two EFIS panels (the display controls beside it) can be clicked in the 3D cockpit, and the lamps follow the modes. A small FCU also sits in the outside views and is clickable there too.
  • The FCU now behaves as the real one does, checked against Airbus references. A turned V/S value waits 45 seconds before you pull it. Pushing the heading knob with no route is refused. Turning the altitude knob alone no longer starts a climb: pull it for an open climb, push it for a managed one. Every knob has a push key and a pull key. See What each control does.
  • Autothrust follows the levers, and SPD/MACH is real. On the ground, moving the thrust levers into TOGA or FLX arms the autothrust without the button (A/THR in light blue). It starts working when you bring the levers back to CL after takeoff (LVR CLB flashes at 1,500 ft), and levers at idle in flight switch it off. The SPD/MACH button now changes the target between knots and a Mach number, and the autothrust holds that Mach as you climb or descend (the FMA says MACH). The FMA draws the white and amber boxes round the manual thrust words. See What each control does.
  • Landing system on the PFD. With LS on, the ident, the tuned frequency and the DME show at the bottom left of the primary flight display. A needle more than two dots off the centre shows as a half diamond at the end of the scale.
  • The airliner tunes the ILS by itself. NAV2 goes to the ILS of your arrival runway (or of the runway you are lined up to), so LOC and APPR work at every ILS runway, not only the one on 108.10 MHz. The MCDU has a RAD NAV page to see it or tune your own, and the Cessna has an Auto-tune ILS option. See Auto-tuning the ILS.
  • Gear. The airliner's gear no longer detaches in a hard bank.
  • Cockpit. The airliner's cockpit stays steady at altitude, and its placard text is sharper. See Cameras.
  • Cloud stays off the cockpit. Cloud no longer draws over the inside of the cockpit, the panel and the frame, when you fly through or beside it. There is no longer an "Entering cloud" or "Leaving cloud" card: the view and the instruments show it. The wind shear card stays. See Wind, cloud and rain.
  • Overspeed is closer to the real aircraft. A couple of seconds over the never-exceed or flap limit is written up for an inspection. Damage needs the design dive speed, or about 15 % over the flap limit, and the airliner's CONF 1 has its own 230 kt limit. See Landing and the airframe.
  • Airliner warnings. OVERSPEED also covers the flap and gear limits. 1+F goes to CONF 1 by itself above 210 kt. A red warning chimes until you press MASTER WARN (Ctrl+M). See The flight deck.

Sound

  • Every system is heard: on the airliner the hydraulic pumps, the PTU, the APU, the packs, the avionics fan and the warning chimes; on the Cessna the turn-coordinator gyro and an engine that works harder under throttle; on both, flap and gear airflow, turbulence, tyres on grass and squealing brakes. See Sound.
  • Outside views hear the aircraft quieter the further away they are, and a fly-by hears its pitch drop. Thunder follows lightning.
  • Airports: the pushback tug and the follow-me car run their engines, the ground power unit and the fuel truck hum, and jet bridges whine and beep while they move.

Routes and navigation

  • Published SIDs and STARs, with their limits. The Route step and the airliner's MCDU offer the departure for your runway and the arrival for your arrival runway, from the Airports Authority of India: every runway of Mumbai and Navi Mumbai, and Pune's SIDs (Pune publishes no STAR; its arrivals are vectored). The airliner's managed climb and descent stop at each altitude limit (ALT CST) and keep under each speed limit, and the top of descent allows for them. The MCDU and the ND show the limits, magenta when met and amber when missed. See Route and A route.
  • Real ILS data. Sixteen runway ends at ten airports now have the ILS the Airports Authority of India publishes, with the real ident, frequency and category (Mumbai, Navi Mumbai, Pune, Belagavi, Kochi, Kozhikode, Goa Mopa and Dabolim, Kannur, Mangaluru). Shivamogga and Sindhudurg keep a simulated one. Approach lights, touchdown-zone lights and bars follow the ends that have an ILS. The table is in Which airports and runways have an ILS. ./run.sh fetches the data on its first run.
  • A Route step on the start screen: auto-route (Direct, VOR to VOR, Airways), a typed route, a nav log, a fuel estimate, a vertical profile with ground clearance, and warnings. Details in Route.
  • Saved plans, and .PLN import and export for Microsoft Flight Simulator and Little Navmap flight plans.
  • Start on a leg of your route in the air, instead of on final.
  • Change the route in flight. On the moving map (N) press Edit route or E. Or press Esc and choose Edit route. See Changing the route in flight and The moving map.
  • The airliner's MCDU is filled in for you by a planned route: route, runways, cruise level, FCU altitude and NAV. See A flight with the autopilot.

Airports and runways

  • Realistic runways. ICAO markings, black tyre rubber where airliners land, sealed cracks, patches, faded paint, and a new look for taxiways. Each runway is worn in its own way. See What both share and Graphics.
  • Your own touchdown marks. Your landings leave fresh tyre marks. They stay in saved flights. A new flight or Restart (F5) clears them.
  • Concrete runways. A runway whose airport data says concrete is drawn as slabs. After you update, run ./run.sh again so the runway step runs and picks this up.
  • Softer apron floodlights, and lit jetways at night. See Lighting.
  • Runways and taxiways are built only near you. Far airports cost nothing until you come close.

Graphics

  • Edge smoothing in Options, Graphics: Auto, SMAA, FXAA or Off. Runway paint, windows and instrument text stay sharper. See Graphics.
  • Rain and ground effects. Rain splashes and rings on wet pavement, wheel spray, propeller wash and dust.
  • Trees and grass of the region. Palms, banyan, teak and thorn scrub, up close and in far forests, with shadows. Grass and undergrowth near the ground.
  • Sharper pictures. High and Ultra are sharp again, and Auto resolution now works on Macs. Changing the renderer offers to restart for you.
  • A shadow on every preset. Your aircraft casts a shadow on Low too, and keeps it on Medium when the camera is far back. Every preset darkens the ground softly under the aircraft. See Graphics.
  • Medium stays sharp. Auto resolution no longer lowers the picture when the computer's processor, not its graphics chip, is what holds the frame rate back. The A320 cockpit uses less video memory.
  • Cheaper clouds on Low and Medium, truer clouds everywhere. Low and Medium now draw cloud at half resolution, as they were meant to. Before, they drew every pixel, the most expensive way. On every preset from Low to Max, clouds keep their dark bases and the gaps between them. Before, cloud drawn at half resolution came out flat and bright grey.
  • Very old graphics chips. The OpenGL fallback now draws clouds and vehicles. See Requirements.
  • Darker, truer dawn and dusk. Sunrise is no longer lit almost like midday: no stars at sunrise, no glowing cirrus before the sun reaches them, and a weak low sun. See Sun, moon and night.

Weather

  • Turbulence levels: None, Light, Moderate and Severe. Gusts rock the aircraft, more on the Cessna than on the airliner.
  • Storms and the Ghats. Storm updrafts and rain shafts, thermals under cumulus, ridge lift on the windward side of the Western Ghats, and mountain waves with a rotor downwind. Ice forms in storm clouds.
  • Pause freezes the weather, the clouds, the clock and any ice. Details in Wind, cloud and rain and Icing.
  • Pause freezes the aircraft too. It no longer shakes behind the pause screen or after a crash. The battery, fuel and gyros wait while you are paused.

Loading and speed

  • Fly is faster. The flight starts loading while you are still choosing. The loading screen shows only the film, and holds the flight screens back until it lifts. See The wait.
  • Fewer draw calls and less memory, from building runways only nearby, and cheaper sky lighting and instruments. Options for a slow computer are in Graphics.

Keys and install

  • Captain Pack map. A picture of the Thrustmaster sidestick and quadrant on the Controls page shows what every key, lever and switch does for the Cessna or the airliner, lights up the control you move, binds any control to any action with one click (it tells you what it replaced), and can identify buttons the map does not know yet. See the Captain Pack map.
  • New keys. E edits the route while the map is open. Each FCU knob has a pull key and a push key (Shift+Alt plus the turn key). Ctrl+Backspace disconnects the autopilot (the sidestick button) and Ctrl+Shift+Backspace the autothrust (the buttons on the thrust levers). FD and LS are actions with no default key: bind them on the Controls page. All are in the key table.
  • MASTER WARN / CAUT is Ctrl+M on the airliner.
  • Quitting while recording (F8) no longer crashes the game on a Mac. The recording is finished properly first.
  • macOS 14 (Sonoma) or newer for the Mac build. See Release packages.
  • After an update, ./run.sh re-imports the game's scripts by itself when it finds new ones, so a missing-class error at launch no longer needs a manual import.

Quick start

In the project folder, double-click Start libresim.desktop (Linux) or Start libresim.command (macOS). The first launch sets the machine up, asks before any sudo, then opens the game. Later launches just open it. On GNOME, right-click the file once and choose Allow Launching.

From a terminal:

git clone <repository> libresim && cd libresim
./run.sh
  • The first run prints a plan: downloads in GB, which pipeline steps will run, and how long to expect. You confirm before it starts. A full first run is hours (about 2.5 GB; imagery alone can take 30–60 minutes). Later runs start in seconds.
  • A step is skipped when its outputs are already newer than its scripts. Editing a comment does not rebuild anything.
  • Progress is a count, a size, and a heartbeat every 15 seconds. The log is data/run.log.
  • Missing packages are detected for apt, dnf, pacman and Homebrew. Godot 4.7.2 is offered into ~/.local/bin if it is missing. Blender is not needed to fly.
  • Game options pass straight through: ./run.sh --airport=VABB --stand.
run.sh

The two aircraft you fly are already in the repository. --a320 is only for regenerating the airliner's flight-model JSON from upstream. A withdrawable archive of just that aircraft is tools/package/make_pack.sh.

GEN 3Start a flight

After the splash, the home screen asks what you want to do. Free flight and Training are the two cards. 1 / 2 or the arrow keys move, Enter confirms. Handbook, Credits and Quit sit in the top bar.

Home

  • Fly again (R) starts your last free-flight setup, after the same check as Fly now.
  • Continue (C) starts the next training lesson, with full guidance. The card shows each lesson's stars. Training is the Cessna only: if the airliner was selected, the lesson switches to the C172.
  • Saved flights, Controls and Options are the links under the cards.

Free flight

The left rail is the order of the flight, and the aircraft comes first because the airport page depends on it:

  1. Aircraft. Specs, registration, fuel and payload.
  2. Livery. Optional. Next skips it.
  3. Airport and start.
  4. Route. Optional. The waypoints you want to fly.
  5. Weather and time.
  6. Failures. Optional.
  7. Recording. Optional.

Each row shows its current value and a mark: green ready, amber look at this, red a problem, hollow optional. Fly now is in the bottom bar from any step. It turns amber Fly anyway when the setup has a problem. Keys 1–7 jump (not while you are typing), Page Up / Page Down step through, Ctrl+Enter flies, Esc goes back. The last setup is user://last_flight.json.

Fly anywayFly asks, and never blocks, when you are over max takeoff weight, the centre of gravity is outside the envelope, the runway is shorter than the aircraft's minimum (runway starts only), the tailwind is above 5 kt, or the crosswind is above the aircraft's limit (C172 15 kt, A320 33 kt). Gusts do not count. Wind is checked for runway and air starts, not for a stand. Visibility under 1.6 km is only a warning. Go back has the focus. Lessons and saved flights skip the question.

Aircraft and the call sign

  • The list is every game/aircraft/<id>/aircraft.json. Today that is the Cessna 172S and the A320-family airliner.
  • Registration is painted on the aircraft. Left alone, it follows the aircraft: VT-LBR or VT-ZZA. A typed one (3 to 7 letters or digits, one optional dash, such as VT-ABC or N172SP) stays when you change aircraft. --callsign= sets it too. On the Cessna that registration is also the radio call. On the airliner the radio stays Skyline 123 unless what you typed is itself an airline call, such as Skyline 456. The same field is under Options → Radio / ATC. VT-FSM is only the fallback when neither you nor the aircraft names one.
  • C172Fuel per tank (default 17 US gal each), front seats, rear seats and baggage (up to 54 kg). The pilot is 81.6 kg. The chart is the normal and utility envelope in inches and pounds. Landing assumes 5 gal a tank.
  • A320Fuel is one total in kilograms (wing tanks first, then the centre tank). Cabin and cargo are separate. The chart is kilograms and percent of mean aerodynamic chord, and it is marked approximate.

Livery

Paint is per aircraft and is remembered with the setup. Presets stay on this computer, under user://liveries/.

  • C172Body, main accent, pin stripe, and struts. Two picture slots: fuselage side and fin.
  • A320Fuselage, fin, cheat line and engines. Wings, slats, flaps, spoilers and the tailplane stay the factory white. Picture slots: fuselage side and fin.

A picture can be PNG, JPG or WebP, up to 4 MB. It appears on the aircraft as you place it. You can change its size, slide it sideways and up, and mirror it to the other side. Reset returns the factory paint and removes pictures. --livery= paints a command-line flight and does not save.

Airport and start

  • Search by ICAO, name or city. Every word must match. Airports that are too short for the aircraft are dimmed.
  • The diagram is north-up. Click a runway end or a stand.
  • Runway. Engine running (both engines, on the airliner). One chip per end shows length and the wind, and turns red when Fly would ask. Tick cold and dark to start with everything off.
  • Stand or gate. Always cold and dark, parking brake set. Real OpenStreetMap refs are kept. A stand without one becomes Gate n or Bay n. Lists put gates first for the airliner and bays or tie-downs first for the Cessna.
  • In the air. Height and distance start at that aircraft's approach and follow the aircraft until you edit one. Default puts them back. Speed has no slider: it is the aircraft's own, unless --air-kt or a lesson sets it.

Route

Plan where you want to go before you fly. The route starts at the airport and start you chose on the Airport step and ends at a destination. Skip the step and there is no route; a direct-to on the moving map still works in flight.

  • To searches for the destination. The runway chips (Any, or one end) add a fix 8 nm out on that runway's final, and on the airliner they set the arrival runway whose ILS the flight computer tunes (the chips do not say which ends have an ILS: see the list). Cruise ft is the altitude new waypoints get: 4,500 ft, or FL240 on the airliner.
  • Auto-route. Direct flies straight there. VOR to VOR hops between radio beacons; a short trip stays direct. Airways takes the shortest path along the published airways, and respects one-way airways. Airways near Mumbai and Pune are few, so on a short trip Direct or VOR to VOR is usually what you want. An amber line says why when a route cannot be built. Clear starts again.
  • Type a route in the box and press Enter: VABB DCT PAPRI W15 GOA VAPO. Spaces or slashes, DCT is ignored, an airway between two of its fixes is filled in. A mistake keeps the old route.
  • On the map: click an airport, beacon or fix to add it. Click empty map for your own waypoint (WPT01, WPT02 …). Shift-click an airport to make it the destination. Drag a waypoint to move it, or drag the middle of a leg to add one there. Right-click a waypoint to remove it. The wheel zooms, dragging empty map pans, F fits the route.
  • Nav log: course, distance, time at the aircraft's cruise speed (C172 124 kt, A320 450 kt), running total and an altitude for every waypoint. ▲ ▼ reorder, × deletes. The total is in still air.
  • Fuel, under the total: Trip 5 gal + reserve 3 gal · on board 34 gal. The trip is the time at cruise speed times the cruise burn (9 gal an hour, 3 gal reserve2,500 kg an hour, 1,800 kg reserve). Red means the fuel you loaded on the Aircraft step does not cover trip and reserve; amber means you would land with less than twice the reserve. It is still air with no climb allowance, so keep a margin.
  • Vertical profile, the strip under the map: the ground along the route and the altitude you planned for each leg. Where a leg is less than 1,000 ft above the ground, the line turns red. The first and last 2 nm are your take-off and landing and are not judged. The caption gives the highest ground and your lowest clearance.
  • Save plan… keeps the route, its altitudes and the departure under a name. Load plan… lists your plans; loading one that starts at another airport moves your departure there too, and the note line tells you. Import .PLN… opens a flight plan from Microsoft Flight Simulator or Little Navmap. Beacons and fixes libresim knows become those points; other points inside the map become your own waypoints, and points outside the map are left out, with a note naming them. The departure must be an airport libresim has. Export .PLN… writes your route for those programs.
  • Start on a leg. With an In the air start, choose On final or On leg N. On a leg you start 2 nm past its first point, heading along it, at its altitude (at least 1,000 ft above the ground ahead), clean, at cruise speed (C172 110 kt, A320 280 kt above 10,000 ft). The airliner starts with the thrust levers in CL and autothrust on, so engaging the autopilot flies the route.

The Route step turns red, and Fly asks "Fly anyway?", when a leg is planned below the ground or the fuel will not cover trip and reserve. It turns amber for a leg less than 1,000 ft above the ground, a thin fuel margin, or a destination runway too short for your aircraft.

In the flight the route is your GPS route: on the moving map, the mini-map and the airliner's F-PLN and ND. The next waypoint becomes active within 1 nm. The Cessna has no autopilot: follow the magenta line and the GPS bearing. On the airliner the MCDU INIT page is already filled with the route, runways and cruise level, and the FCU altitude is set and NAV is selected, so the autopilot flies it once you engage it. The controller's clearance names your destination. Saves keep the route; Restart (F5) starts it again. Lessons never use it.

SID and STAR. Under the destination row you can pick a published departure (SID) for your start runway and a published arrival (STAR) for your arrival runway. Mumbai and Navi Mumbai have both, from the Airports Authority of India's 2026 supplements; Pune has SIDs only, because its arrivals are radar vectored in reality, so the sim keeps its own arrival points there. A SID needs a runway start. Their fixes join the route at the start and the end, with their altitude and speed limits, which the airliner's managed climb and descent respect (see A route). None keeps the sim's own runway fixes. For example, from Pune runway 28 the SID OPAKA2A and Mumbai's STAR OPAKA3K to runway 27 chain into one published route.

Changing the route in flight. Two ways, both starting from where you are now, at the start of the leg you are flying:

  • On the moving map (N) press Edit route, or E (the button and the hint line show the key; it works only while the map is open, and you can rebind it on the Controls page as "Map: edit route"). The map gets a magenta frame and the route ahead takes the same clicks as the Route step: click to add a point before the destination, drag a waypoint to move it, drag a leg to add a point on it (the leg from your aircraft too: that point is then flown first), right-click to remove, Shift-click an airport to make it the destination. Every change is flown at once. The wheel still zooms and dragging empty map still pans. Closing the map ends edit mode.
  • Press Esc and choose Edit route: the Route step's map, nav log, profile and fuel line (with the fuel you have now), plus the destination, its runway, cruise altitude, auto-route and the text box. Nothing changes until Apply and resume; Cancel or Esc drops the edits. The plan buttons are there too. Save plan… and Export .PLN… keep the whole route, the waypoints you have already flown included. Load plan… and Import .PLN… replace only the route ahead of you: your present position stays the start, the plan's waypoints behind you are left out, and nothing flies until you Apply. A plan that starts at another airport brings its waypoints and destination, but your departure airport does not change in the air; the note line tells you.

The GPS keeps the waypoint it is flying to while that waypoint is still the next point; if you removed or moved it, the next point ahead takes over. The mini-map, the ND and the controller's destination follow. The MCDU F-PLN shows the new route, INIT takes the new cruise level (CRZ FL) and keeps your departure runway, and NAV keeps flying it; your FCU altitude is not touched. Waypoints you have already passed stay in the plan, so a save or a restart flies the edited route.

Sound

Everything you hear follows the aircraft's state, so a sound tells you something. Options → Sound has a slider for each group: engine, wind and environment, cockpit, radio and menus.

  • Engines. The Cessna's engine follows RPM and sounds harder under full throttle than at the same RPM in a dive. Starter, catch and cut are their own sounds, and a windmilling propeller still turns over quietly. Each airliner engine is heard on its own side: idle and power by N1, the whine by N2, the start, the shutdown and reverse. The APU spools up and runs down with its speed.
  • Systems. The vacuum gyros and the electric turn coordinator spin up and run down, the avionics fan hums, the flap motor and the trim wheel run while they move, and the stall horn sounds when the wing is near the stall. Hydraulic pumps, the PTU barking when it transfers pressure, the packs, the avionics fan, the gear and the flap and slat motors, and the gear locking up or down. The master warning chimes until you press MASTER WARN (Ctrl+M); a caution chimes once. Overhead and FCU buttons click.
  • Air and ground. Wind grows with airspeed. Extended flaps and gear add their own rush. Rough air and storm cells shake the airframe and you hear it. Rain drums on the windshield. The tyres roll differently on grass than on the runway, and the brakes squeal at walking pace.
  • Outside. From an outside view the aircraft is quieter the further away the camera is, and its pitch drops as it passes the fly-by camera. Thunder follows lightning a few seconds later, as far away as the strike. At an airport the pushback tug and the follow-me car run their engines, the ground power unit and the fuel truck pump hum while the ramp crew is out, and a jet bridge whines and beeps while it moves.

Pause quiets the flight; menu sounds stay. A missing sound file is silent rather than an error.

  2. Open the checklist with F2. The lists are Before Starting Engine, Starting Engine, Before Takeoff (run-up), Normal Takeoff, Enroute Climb, Cruise, Before Landing, Normal Landing, After Landing and Securing Airplane. Items the sim can read tick themselves. Click an item, or press Enter, to tick the others. Ctrl+Page Up / Page Down change list. See Checklists.

  5. Taxi. Shift+B releases the parking brake. T draws the taxi route and sends the follow-me car. Steer with Z and X. At the hold, do the run-up list: about 1,800 RPM, a magneto check, vacuum and engine gauges. Then strobes and landing light on. See Taxi and takeoff.

The whole approach, step by step, is in Flying an ILS in the Cessna. The runways that have one are in the table.

Cold start

A stand start, a runway start with Cold and dark ticked, and --cold begin here. Parking brake is set. Battery, alternator, avionics and lights are off. Mixture is idle cutoff, throttle closed, magnetos OFF. Fuel is already on BOTH and the shutoff is open. The altimeter reads 29.92 inHg, which is 1013 hPa, not whatever QNH you picked. A runway start without Cold and dark skips this: the engine is running and the altimeter is already set.

The switches are the bottom row of the panel, or these keys. Levers move while you hold the key, about half their travel per second.

  1. Shift+M. Battery and alternator on. The starter does not turn on a dead battery.
  2. Hold ' for about two seconds. Mixture full rich. ; is lean, all the way to idle cutoff.
  3. Tap Page Up. Throttle cracked, not open. Page Down closes it.
  4. M three times. Magnetos go OFF, then L, then R, then BOTH. One more M from BOTH is OFF again.
  5. Ctrl+B. Beacon on.
  6. Hold S. It cranks, catches after about a second, and idles when you release S.
  7. Oil pressure comes off zero. Shift+V turns the avionics, and the radios, on.
  8. Set the altimeter. Click either half of the Kollsman knob, 0.01 inHg a click, Shift for 0.10. 0.01 inHg is about 0.34 hPa.

Taxi and takeoff

  2. Shift+B to release the brake. T draws the taxi route to the into-wind runway and sends the follow-me; T again clears it. Steer with Z and X. Hold B to brake. , and . are the toe brakes.
  3. At the runway: Ctrl+S strobes, Ctrl+L landing light. Page Up to full throttle, about 2,300 RPM. Hold right rudder (X). The aeroplane pulls left.
  4. At 55 kt, ease ↓ and rotate. Climb at 74 kt. In the climb, hold a little right aileron as well. Above 500 ft, flaps up if you used 10°.
  5. Level off, trim with Home (nose down) and End (nose up) until it holds, then about 2,400 RPM. For cruise, lean with ;. Hands-off after a minute is about 10° left at 90 KIAS and about level at 100. Up through 110 KIAS the bank stays inside about 10°.

  10. Land. Gear down (Ctrl+G), flaps FULL or CONF 3, and a stable approach near the approach speed. On an ILS approach with both autopilots, gear down and CONF 3 or FULL, the aircraft lands itself (LAND, FLARE, ROLL OUT). Without that the autopilot lets go at 50 ft and you flare and land by hand. Ground spoilers come out by themselves once both levers are at idle. Use reverse and the autobrake. See Autoland and Landing and parking.
  11. Park and shut down. Vacate, speedbrake in, flaps up, lights off. At the gate: brake set, engine masters off, then the Parking and Securing lists. See Landing and parking.

What is modelled

Two FADEC turbofans, slats and flaps, spoilers, a trimmable stabiliser, retractable gear, nosewheel steering, anti-skid and autobrake. Displays: PFD, ND, engine and warning, systems, FCU, lite MCDU, on the 3D panel and on the Full and Split cards outside.

Not modelled: a predicted climb and descent path (VNAV; the SID and STAR limits are flown by levelling at them), the STARs of airports that publish none (Pune), faults on the overhead buttons, or the overhead systems beyond electrics, hydraulics, fuel, bleed and packs. Speeds

Callout speeds in the sim are fixed: airspeed alive 80 kt, V1 135, rotate 140. They do not change with weight. Approach is about 135 KIAS and does change with weight. VMO / MMO are 350 KIAS / M 0.82. The data set's design dive speed is 380 KIAS. Fly anyway asks above 33 kt of crosswind. A runway shorter than about 1,800 m is dimmed, and it is never the default.

1 g stall speeds in this data set, near 64 t: clean 155 kt, CONF 1 about 129, 1+F about 122, CONF 2 about 115, CONF 3 about 113, FULL about 109. The speed tape shows the limit for the flap lever where it is. Load factor beyond about +2.5 / −1.0 g is the airframe limit.

How it flies

In normal law the sidestick asks for a load factor in pitch and a roll rate in roll. Let go in roll and it holds the bank. Pitch trim follows you. Protections cover angle of attack, high speed, bank, pitch and load factor. A failure can drop the law to alternate or direct. Below about 50 ft the flare law holds the attitude you have set (it stands aside when the autopilot lands the aircraft: the autoland flares through its own load factor demand). On the ground the spoilers stay out through a bounce, and the slats lead the flaps when you move the lever.

Keyboard pitch and roll are the sidestick. Page Up and Page Down move both thrust levers. Alt+Page Up / Page Down are engine 1 alone, Alt+Home / End engine 2. Alt+= / − step the detent. Reverse is Ctrl+X or Num *. Speedbrake out is \, in is /. Gear is Ctrl+G. Autobrake cycles with Ctrl+A.

Cold start

A stand or gate, and a runway start with Cold and dark ticked, begin here. Batteries are already on, so the displays work. The battery, generator, fuel pump, hydraulic, bleed and pack buttons and the sign switches are on the overhead: click them in the cockpit, or give them a key on the Controls page (they have none by default). Most light OFF when switched off. F2 Cockpit Preparation walks through them. Engines are off, masters off, engine mode NORM, thrust at idle, parking brake set, APU off, lights off. A runway start without Cold and dark has both engines running. Skip to taxi.

The engines start on battery air. The APU is optional.

  1. APU, if you want it: Ctrl+U master on, then hold Alt+U for a moment. The start latches. It is available in about 30 seconds. Ctrl+U again shuts it down. Click APU BLEED on the overhead for start air, as the checklist asks; the sim's engines also start without it.
  2. Ctrl+I twice. Engine mode goes NORM, then CRANK, then IGN/START. Leave it on IGN/START. CRANK only motors an engine whose master is off, and does not light it. One more Ctrl+I from IGN/START is NORM.
  3. Ctrl+D. Engine 2 master on. You do not hold it. N2 rises on its own. Light-off is about 20 seconds. Idle is about 45 seconds, near 19% N1 and 59% N2.
  4. Ctrl+E. Engine 1 master on. Same wait. Either engine can be first.
  5. Ctrl+I once, back to NORM. Check GEN 1 and GEN 2 show no OFF light. APU BLEED off, then APU off with Ctrl+U if you no longer need it.

If an engine does not light: mode is IGN/START, that master is on, and there is fuel. A failed engine or an engine fire will not start. The same controls are the knob and the master levers on the pedestal. They show the position you set.

Taxi and takeoff

  1. Flaps for takeoff: ] once is CONF 1, slats out and flaps still in, on the ground and in the air. ] again is 1+F. A third press is CONF 2. [ retracts one detent. 1+F goes to CONF 1 on its own above 210 kt, as on the real aircraft. On the Captain Pack the first notch is 1+F on the ground and CONF 1 in the air.
  2. Ctrl+A cycles autobrake OFF, LO, MED, MAX. Use MAX for takeoff. Speedbrake in with /. \ walks it out. Ctrl+B beacon, Ctrl+N nav lights.
  3. At a gate, before pushback: Shift+J retracts the jet bridge. It shuts the L1 door, folds the canopy and drives the bridge back. Close any other door: Shift+O for all passenger doors, Ctrl+O for both cargo doors. Pushback waits until the bridge is back and every door is shut, and says which key is missing.
  4. Shift+B releases the parking brake. At a gate, P pushes back and swings the tail toward the taxiway, then stops before a wheel leaves the pavement. The brake has to be off. P again stops. T is the taxi route and the follow-me. Stay under 30 kt.
  5. Steer with Z and X. That is the rudder pedals, about 6° of nosewheel, fading out by about 130 kt. A tiller axis on a joystick steers further and fades by about 70 kt. The Controls page lists it: bind the axis, or give Tiller left and Tiller right a key. Hold Alt+Y (Pedals disconnect, the button on the tiller; a joystick button works too) and the pedals move only the rudder, not the nosewheel: that is how you check the rudder while taxiing. Ctrl+T is the taxi light.
  6. Lined up: Ctrl+S strobes, Ctrl+L landing lights. Alt+= steps both thrust levers IDLE, CL, FLX/MCT, TOGA. Takeoff is FLX/MCT or TOGA. Page Up moves both levers smoothly. Alt+Page Up / Page Down is engine 1. Alt+Home / End is engine 2.

An air start is already set up: 7 nm out, 2,200 ft above the field, 160 KIAS, gear down, CONF 2. --air-kt at 200 or more raises the gear and cleans up.

Landing and parking

  1. Ctrl+L landing lights. Ctrl+G gear down. Ctrl+A to LO or MED.
  2. Flaps: from clean, ] four times is CONF 3 and five times is FULL. The checklist allows CONF 3. Stabilise near the approach speed on the tape, about 135 kt and it does change with weight.
  3. Leave the speedbrake lever in. Ground spoilers come out on their own above about 30 kt with both levers at idle or in reverse, and they stay out through a bounce until a lever goes above idle. Reverse: from idle, hold Ctrl+X or Num *. Release it and the levers return to idle.

At a jetway the bridge reaches the L1 door in about 6 seconds once you are parked within 3 m. Then its canopy comes down onto the fuselage, and the L1 door opens. The open door lies inside the canopy. Leaving runs the other way: door shut, canopy up, bridge back. Shift+J retracts the bridge before pushback, and docks it again. A bridge retracted for pushback docks again at the arrival gate. It is not solid while it is extended. Limits are in OPS 9.

The doors open the Airbus way, and only stopped on the ground. A passenger door lifts off its stops, swings out and forward on its hinge arm, and ends flat along the fuselage ahead of the doorway. Its inside handle goes up as it unlocks. The slide stays disarmed, with the safety pin and its red streamer in. The cargo doors on the lower right swing up. O is door L1, Shift+O all passenger doors, and Ctrl+O both cargo doors. R1, L2, R2 and each cargo door have their own row under Ground operations on the Controls page; give them a key there. The flight deck

  • PFD. Attitude, speed tape with the flap limit, altitude, heading, flight-mode annunciations.
  • ND. Five modes, set with the mode knob on the EFIS panel (it turns LS, VOR, NAV, ARC, PLAN), or by clicking or scrolling the mode name at the top left of the ND. ARC and ROSE NAV show the route and the map. ROSE VOR shows VOR 1's course pointer and deviation bar in light blue, with a TO / FROM arrow. ROSE LS shows the ILS in magenta, with the glide slope scale on the right. Both list the receiver, frequency, course, ident and DME at the bottom right, and leave the map off, as the real one does. PLAN is north up and centred on the next waypoint, for checking the route. Weather radar paints in every mode but PLAN when there is a return, marked WX.
  • MCDU. A long route fits: INIT shows only origin and destination under FROM/TO, and F-PLN scrolls six lines at a time with the mouse wheel or the UP / DN keys under the letters. They light while there is more of the route that way.
  • E/WD and SD. Engine, fuel, wheel and flight-control pages. ICE DETECTED and ANTI ICE show on the memo line. A red warning sounds the continuous chime and an amber one a single chime; Ctrl+M (MASTER WARN / CAUT) silences them while the lines stay on the screen, until a new warning comes. There is no glareshield button for it yet. There is no schematic flight-control page, and no bleed or pack model.
  • FCU and EFIS panels. The Flight Control Unit (FCU) is the strip of knobs and buttons across the glareshield. It is the autopilot panel. The two EFIS panels beside it hold FD, LS, BARO and the ND controls. Every button and knob works, in the 3D cockpit and on the small FCU in the outside views. How to use them, three ways, is in Flying with the autopilot; what each one does is in What each control does.
  • Reading the autopilot. The mode display across the top of the PFD is the FMA: Reading the FMA.

The overhead's electrics, hydraulics, fuel pumps, bleeds and packs, and the seat belt and no smoking signs are switches you click in the cockpit. Most show OFF when switched off. External power, the yellow electric pump, crossfeed and APU bleed show ON instead. They have no keys by default; bind them on the Controls page if you want. Buttons for systems the sim does not model stay still.

Lights beyond the usual set: Alt+T take-off, Alt+R runway turn-off, Alt+W wing and engine scan, Alt+O logo. Wing anti-ice is Ctrl+W. Engine anti-ice is Alt+E and Alt+D. Probe heat is automatic. Anti-ice uses bleed air and costs a little thrust.

A route

The MCDU is on the pedestal, and on the Full and Split cards outside. Scratchpad keys work when the pointer is over that unit.

  • INIT FROM/TO files a route: VABB/BBB/VAPO is origin, optional vias, destination. An ident that is not an airport, a VOR, an NDB, a published airway fix or a synthetic runway fix is rejected.
  • A designator between two fixes that lie on it expands to the published points. VABB/BBB/W56/BIA/VOBL flies W56 from BBB to BIA, from the Airports Authority of India eAIP. The entry and the exit have to be on that airway.
  • DEP RWY and ARR RWY insert a fix 5 nm ahead of the departure runway and a fix 8 nm out on the arrival centreline (VABB27D, VAPO10F). They are the sim's own points, not a SID or a STAR.
  • SID (left key 6) and STAR (right key 6) on INIT: press with an empty scratchpad to step through the procedures published for DEP RWY or ARR RWY (after the last one, none), or type a name such as PPN3A and press. The route is filed again: the SID's fixes replace the 5 nm departure fix, the STAR's come before the final fix. F-PLN shows each limit under its fix, for example +6000, -FL120, FL110 or 230/+FL120 (speed / altitude), magenta while it is met and amber when it is missed. On the ND a fix with a limit is circled in the same colours; press CSTR on the EFIS panel to see the values.
  • F-PLN edits a leg. DIR skips to a waypoint already in the plan or inserts one and then resumes it.
  • RAD NAV shows VOR1 (frequency and course, typed with L1 and R1) and the ILS (L2). The ILS is the one the flight computer tuned for the arrival runway: ident, frequency and course, marked AUTO. A frequency you type on L2 is MANUAL and wins. Type AUTO and press L2 to give it back. See Auto-tuning the ILS.
  • Filing or a direct-to selects NAV. The heading window shows dashes. Pull the heading knob (Shift+click, or Shift+Alt+→) to go back to a selected heading. With the autopilot on, NAV flies the leg and sequences within 1 nm.
  • Cruise flight level on INIT is copied to the FCU altitude and selects managed vertical. At top of descent the FCU altitude is lowered to the destination elevation plus 2,200 ft, below the glide slope at the 8 nm final fix. On a SID or STAR the managed climb and descent stop at each altitude limit on the way (ALT CST on the mode display) and go on by themselves once the fix is passed.

The same route can be built on the moving map, which both aircraft share. That is OPS 4. A route planned in the start screen's Route step arrives here already filed.

Flying with the autopilot

The airliner has two autopilots (AP1 and AP2), a flight director (FD) and autothrust (A/THR). The flight director draws bars on the primary flight display (PFD) that show where to point the aircraft. The autopilot flies the aircraft to those bars. Autothrust works the engines for you. You control all three from the Flight Control Unit (FCU), the strip of knobs and buttons across the glareshield.

Two words run through everything:

  • Managed means the aircraft's computer picks the value. Heading follows your route (this is called NAV). Speed follows a simple schedule. The FCU window shows dashes and a dot.
  • Selected means you pick the number, and the aircraft flies it.

The speed, heading, altitude and vertical-speed knobs all work the same way. Turn a knob to set a number. Push it to choose managed (for the altitude knob, a managed climb or descent; for the vertical-speed knob, level off). Pull it to choose selected (for the altitude knob, an open climb or descent; for the vertical-speed knob, fly that rate).

Turning is not enoughTurning a knob only sets the number in its window. It does not change what the aircraft is doing. You must push or pull. The one exception is the vertical-speed knob while V/S is already flying: a turn changes the rate at once.

SidestickMoving the sidestick takes the aircraft back from the autopilot. The keyboard pitch and roll keys and a joystick move the sidestick, so touching them disconnects it. The red takeover button on the sidestick disconnects it too, with a key: Ctrl+Backspace.

The knob moves in or out and springs back, and it does the same when you push or pull it with a key or on the small FCU. Point at a knob and turn the mouse wheel to turn it: one step for each notch, up is more, with the same steps as dragging (the ALT knob follows its 100 / 1000 selector, the heading wraps at 359). On a trackpad, about 12 points of scroll is one step. Away from a knob the wheel zooms the view, as before, and a pinch always zooms. Point at a control and a note names it and shows its key and its state. FD, LS, BARO and the ND controls are on the EFIS panels beside the FCU.
  • On the small FCU in the outside views. Press Tab until the Full layout shows. The FCU sits along the top of the panel. On the Custom layout, turn on "FCU (autopilot panel)" under Options, Screen layout; it is off until you do. The Split, Compact and Minimal layouts have no FCU. Click the buttons. On each knob (SPD, HDG, ALT, V/S), turn the mouse wheel over its label or window for one step, or drag up or down to turn it. Click it to push, Shift+click to pull. A flight with the autopilot, step by step

This flight goes from takeoff to an ILS landing. Keys are in brackets. The detail of each control is above.

Before takeoff

  1. Get a route. A route planned on the start screen's Route step is already in the MCDU: route, runways, cruise level. Otherwise file one on the MCDU INIT page (A route). Filing sets the FCU altitude to the cruise level, with a managed climb and descent, and selects NAV. A route from the Route step also selects managed speed.
  2. Check the FCU. The ALT window shows the cruise level with a dot. The HDG window shows dashes and a dot (NAV). In the SPD window, dashes mean managed speed. If it shows a number, you will fly that speed: click the knob (Shift+Alt+[) for managed. Check that FD is on.
  3. Arm the autothrust (Ctrl+3) if you want it. On the ground it shows light blue in column five.

Takeoff and climb

  1. Set takeoff thrust: FLX/MCT or TOGA (Alt+= steps the detents). The FMA shows MAN TOGA (or FLX), SRS and RWY, and the A/THR light comes on: the autothrust is armed, not working yet. Rotate at 140 kt, following the pitch bar, and raise the gear.
  2. Engage AP1 (Ctrl+1) once you are more than 100 ft up. Column five shows AP1.
  3. Reach 1,500 ft above the field. SRS changes to CLB (OP CLB if vertical is not managed). With autothrust armed the thrust column flashes LVR CLB: bring the levers back to CL (Alt+− steps them down a detent). It changes to THR CLB and A/THR turns white: the autothrust now works the engines. Without autothrust the column keeps saying MAN and you set the thrust yourself. This works however you moved the levers up, in one go or a notch at a time. Retract the flaps as the speed reaches the managed target: with flaps out it is the speed at which the next step can come in.
  4. Fly the route. NAV flies the route and moves on to the next waypoint within 1 nm. To fly a heading instead, turn the HDG knob to it and pull. Push the knob to go back to NAV. If the route reaches the final fix from the far side of the airport (Mumbai to Pune runway 28, for example), the flight computer adds a base fix 5 nm to the side of it (VAPO28B or VAPO28A), so the last turn onto the final is a right angle and the localizer is caught in time. Delete it on the F-PLN page if you do not want it.
  5. Climb as you like. The managed climb (CLB) goes to the cruise level at the managed speed. The managed speed is about 250 kt below 10,000 ft, then about 300 kt, and it is held to Mach 0.78. For an open climb instead: turn the ALT knob to the altitude, then pull it (OP CLB). For a fixed rate: turn V/S, then pull it. To stop climbing now, push the V/S knob.
  6. Level off. The FMA goes ALT*, then ALT (ALT CRZ after a managed climb). Turning the ALT knob while in ALT does not move the aircraft.

Descent

  1. Managed descent. With a filed route and managed vertical on (a dot in the ALT window), the sim picks a top of descent for you: the 3 degree rule of about 300 ft per nm, plus 8 nm, plus about 10 nm to slow down on the way (to 250 kt at 10,000 ft, and for the approach). There the FCU altitude drops to the destination's elevation plus 2,200 ft, and the FMA shows DES. That height is below the glide slope at the final fix 8 nm out, so the beam is met from below. A managed descent never climbs to lose speed: it levels off to slow down instead. There are no altitude constraints along the way.
  2. The approach phase. In the last 25 nm of the route (the MCDU PROG page says APPR), or once LOC or APPR is armed below 7,200 ft, the managed speed slows to green dot, about 210 kt. Set the flaps one step at a time as the speed allows: each step slows it further, to the approach speed at FULL. Before this phase it stays at 250 kt, too fast for flaps.
  3. Open descent. Turn the ALT knob to a lower altitude and pull it. The FMA shows OP DES, and with autothrust on, THR IDLE. The pitch holds the speed. Extend the speedbrake (\) if you need to come down faster. Use the same steps for a vertical-speed descent.
  4. Set the pressure with the BARO knob when you pass the transition level, and slow down as ATC asks. The checklist's Approach list covers it.

ILS approach and landing

The ILS has its own chapter, with the runway list, the instruments, the capture table and the autoland: Flying an ILS in the airliner and Autoland. These are the steps in flight order.

  1. Join the beam. Descend to the intercept altitude and hold it (ALT). Use a selected heading (pull HDG) to bring the aircraft toward the localizer course, within about 100 degrees of it. Slow down: turn SPD and pull, or push it for the managed speed, which comes down to the approach speed as you set flaps.
  2. Check that the ILS is received. With LS on, the ident and frequency show at the bottom left of the PFD. The flight computer has tuned your arrival runway's ILS (how it chooses). If nothing shows, the computer has found no ILS for the runway (the runway has none, or LS is off). The scales appear once the signal is received, within 18 nm. You can type another frequency on the MCDU RAD NAV page.
  3. Press APPR (Ctrl+5), and then AP2 (Ctrl+2). The FMA shows LOC armed on line two of column three, and G/S armed on line two of column two. Column five shows AP1+2.
  4. Watch the captures. LOC* then LOC line you up. The glide slope captures when the path reaches you from below, so stay level below it: G/S*, then G/S. It also captures from above if you are less than about one dot high (about 80 m at 7 nm, 23 m at 2 nm). Much higher than that nothing captures: G/S stays light blue until you descend through the path. Put the gear down and set the flaps to CONF 3 or FULL before 400 ft: the autoland needs it, and column four says CAT3 DUAL when it is ready. If the glide slope signal is lost, it flies the vertical speed it had.
  5. Below 400 ft with both autopilots on, the FMA shows LAND in column two: the aircraft will land itself. Keep your hands off the sidestick. At 40 ft it shows FLARE and raises the nose; from 30 ft the autothrust takes the thrust to idle (THR IDLE), and at 10 ft the thrust column says RETARD: pull your own levers to idle then. In a crosswind it turns the nose to the runway just before touchdown. At touchdown column three shows ROLL OUT and the autopilot keeps the centreline with the rudder and the nose wheel. You still have to set the autobrake before you land, pull your levers to idle (the ground spoilers need both at idle or in reverse), and use reverse (Landing and parking). Below 20 kt of ground speed the autopilots let go by themselves; you can disconnect earlier with Ctrl+Backspace. If your levers are still in CL then, the autothrust keeps the engines at idle until you pull them back, and then switches itself off. With only one autopilot, or in CONF 2 or less, there is no LAND: the autopilot disconnects with a tone at 50 ft and you flare by hand. If the localizer is lost above 15 ft or the glide slope above 100 ft the autopilots disconnect with the warning: take over.
  6. Going around. With the flaps out, on the glide slope (also in LAND and FLARE, which then end), push the thrust levers to TOGA. The FMA shows SRS, and the heading you had is held. Clean up the aircraft. At 1,500 ft above the field SRS changes to OP CLB (or CLB when vertical is managed).
  7. After landing turn the autothrust off (Ctrl+3), if it is still armed. The autopilot has already dropped.

Disconnecting

  • Autopilot. Press AP1 or AP2 again (Ctrl+1, Ctrl+2). Or press the sidestick takeover button: Ctrl+Backspace (the Autopilot disconnect action; AP DISC on the small FCU; bindable to a joystick button). The autopilot drops, a tone sounds, AP OFF flashes red in the FMA and AUTO FLT AP OFF shows in red on the engine and warning display. After your own disconnect the warning stops by itself after 1.5 seconds. After any other (the sidestick moved, a failure, power lost) it goes on until you press the takeover button again. Pressing it with no autopilot on also cancels the warning.
  • Sidestick. A small movement of the stick takes over at once.
  • Autothrust. Press A/THR (Ctrl+3), click the disconnect button on a thrust lever in the cockpit, use the Autothrust disconnect action (Ctrl+Shift+Backspace), or bring the thrust levers to idle in flight.
  • By itself. Loss of avionics power, touchdown (not in an autoland), 50 ft on an ILS approach without an autoland, a lost localizer or glide slope during LAND or FLARE, and below 20 kt on the roll out of an autoland.

What the autopilot does not do

  • Autoland is simplified. It needs both autopilots, the ILS, the gear down and CONF 3 or FULL; without them it lets go at 50 ft as before. The real aircraft can also land with one autopilot (CAT 3 SINGLE) and checks the runway's ILS category and many monitors (the red AUTOLAND light, radio altimeter and deviation limits); the sim checks none of that, and it does not enforce the crosswind, head wind and tail wind limits (20, 30 and 10 kt). The flare shape, the heights and the roll out steering are the sim's own tuning from a simulator guide, not Airbus data; the autopilot lets go below 20 kt, where the real one waits for you. There is no RETARD voice call. No CAT 2 line and no DH / NO DH line in column four.
  • A simple flight management computer. It flies the published SIDs and STARs of Mumbai and Navi Mumbai and the SIDs of Pune, with their altitude and speed limits: the managed climb levels at an "at or below" limit until the fix is passed (ALT CST), the managed descent levels at an "at or above" one, the speed stays under each speed limit, and the top of descent comes early enough for the STAR's "at or below" limits. There is no predicted climb or descent path: between limits the aircraft climbs at climb thrust and descends at idle. A SID limit wholly above your cruise level is not flown, as on the real aircraft. Pune publishes no STAR (its arrivals are radar vectored), so the sim keeps its own arrival points there; where a Mumbai STAR ends in radar vectors, the sim flies a "vectors" point (VABB27V) on the published heading, far enough out for the descent, then the final fix. The final fix 8 nm out and the base fix beside it are the sim's own points, not published ones; there is no DECEL point drawn, and the approach phase simply starts 25 nm before the destination. Managed speed is a simple schedule: about 250 kt below 10,000 ft, then about 300 kt in a climb or 280 kt otherwise, never above Mach 0.78; green dot in the approach phase, then each flap step's retraction speed (S, F), and the approach speed at FULL. Top of descent is the rule above.
  • EXPED is not modelled. HDG V/S - TRK FPA and METRIC ALT change only the displays.
  • No automatic changeover between knots and Mach. You press SPD/MACH yourself. The real aircraft switches by itself at a crossover altitude, but no Airbus text for the rule was found, so it is not guessed.
  • Autothrust details left out. Thrust lock (THR LK) when it drops out with the levers in CL, the A/THR LIMITED caution, and the 15 second hold on the instinctive disconnect that switches autothrust off for the flight.
  • Not confirmed against the real aircraft. The 45 s heading and 10 s speed windows. A second press of LOC or APPR disarming it. Whether the vertical-speed window should show a dot. Pressing AP2 while AP1 is on outside an approach (the real aircraft may drop AP1; the sim keeps both). Pushing the ALT knob with no route (the real aircraft would refuse; the sim engages a managed climb or descent anyway). The autothrust arming rules (TOGA or FLX arming it on the ground, idle disconnecting it in flight) come from community and training references, not from Airbus text.
  • Disconnect warnings, simplified. The warning is one red AUTO FLT AP OFF line and a flashing AP OFF in the FMA, with the tone repeating while it lasts. There is no master warning light that flashes and no ECAM clear. Pressing an AP button on the FCU counts as your own disconnect (1.5 seconds); Airbus references suggest that case warns until cancelled. Disconnecting the autothrust gives no warning at all. The lasting AUTO FLT AP FAULT line that follows an Autopilot failure is the sim's own wording.
  • Not drawn. The white box round a changed mode.
  • Flying qualities. The autopilot banks no more than 25 degrees, and it limits how hard it pulls and pushes (about 0.3 g and 0.2 g beyond 1 g), so a level-off or a glide-slope correction is smooth, never sharp. The captain's and the first officer's FD buttons share one switch. The checks against Airbus data are listed in docs/A320-FCU-REFERENCE.md.

An ILS (instrument landing system) is a pair of radio beams that lead you down to a runway you cannot yet see. You fly the beams. At a set height you look up. If you see the runway, you land. If you do not, you go around. This chapter explains the system, lists the runways that have one, and shows how to fly it in each aircraft. It is a simulation for the game, and the data is not for navigation.

What an ILS is

  • The localizer (LOC) is a beam along the runway centreline. It tells you left or right. On the Cessna it is the vertical needle of the dial, which swings sideways. On the airliner's primary flight display (PFD) it is the scale under the attitude sphere.
  • The glide slope (G/S) is a beam that slopes down to the runway at 3 degrees. It tells you whether you are above or below the path. On the Cessna it is the horizontal needle on the NAV2 dial, which swings up and down. On the airliner it is the scale to the right of the sphere. Three degrees is about 320 ft of height for each nautical mile. On the path you are about 3,200 ft above the runway at 10 nm, 1,900 ft at 6 nm, 950 ft at 3 nm and 320 ft at 1 nm.
  • The ident and the frequency. Each ILS has a four-letter ident, such as ICIL, and a frequency between 108 and 112 MHz. You tune the frequency on a navigation radio. The ident shows you that you tuned the right one.
  • The distance. In the sim every ILS also gives the distance to the runway threshold (DME, distance measuring equipment).
  • The category and the decision height. The category says how low a pilot may fly on the beams without seeing the runway. The decision height (DH) is the height at which you must decide: runway in sight, land; not in sight, go around. The usual figures are a DH of 200 ft above the touchdown zone for CAT I and 100 ft for CAT II. CAT III is lower, or none, and lets the aircraft land itself. These are the standard figures, not the sim's. The sim shows the category on the map, but it does not enforce a decision height and it does not check the runway visibility.

How the sim builds one. Each ILS belongs to one runway end. Its course is the runway heading. The sim puts the localizer antenna 300 m beyond the far end of the runway, and the glide slope antenna 300 m past the threshold and 120 m to one side. The path is 3 degrees, unless the AIP remarks say otherwise. The reception rules are these:

  • Localizer: within 18 nm of the antenna and within 35 degrees either side of the centreline, on the approach side. You get nothing from behind the antenna, from the far end of the runway or from beside it. Hills can block it: the terrain is checked twice a second.
  • Glide slope: within 10 nm of its antenna and within 8 degrees of the centreline.
  • Needles: the localizer needle is full scale at about 1.5 degrees off the centreline far out, and at up to 3 degrees close in. The glide slope needle is full scale at 0.7 degrees above or below the path. That is about 220 ft at 3 nm and 75 ft at 1 nm.
  • Two stations on one frequency: the nearer one answers. The OBS knob does nothing to an ILS needle.

Tuning the radios

Cessna

  • The avionics have to be on (Shift+V).
  • On the radio stack, point at the standby frequency of NAV2 (the right-hand box of the second KX-155) and turn the wheel: 50 kHz a step, 1 MHz with Shift. Click the swap arrow to make it active. NAV takes 108.00 to 117.95 MHz. NAV2 starts at 108.10, with 109.30 in standby.
  • Read the ident under the NAV2 dial. If it is not the one you want, you are on the wrong frequency, or a nearer station shares it.
  • Set the OBS to the runway heading if you like. It changes nothing for an ILS.
  • Auto-tune ILS is an option, off by default (Options, Radio / ATC). With it on, NAV2 follows the ILS you are flying to, by the rules in the next part. Turn the NAV2 frequency yourself and your choice wins until you change the destination of your route or restart.

The airliner tunes it for you

  • When. The flight computer tunes NAV2 to the ILS you are flying to, as the real one does for the arrival runway. It does so only in the air, with the avionics on.
  • Which, with a plan. It follows the arrival runway of your plan: the ARR RWY on the MCDU INIT page, or the one you chose on the start screen's Route step. It starts when you are within 100 nm of the destination, or in the descent. With a destination but no arrival runway it takes the runway you are most nearly flying straight in to.
  • Which, with no plan. It tunes the ILS of the runway you are lined up to: an ILS threshold within 25 nm ahead, within 30 degrees of your track and below 6,000 ft above the threshold. Arming LOC or APPR lifts the height limit.
  • What it shows. With LS on, the PFD shows the ident and the frequency at once, before the signal is received. A runway with no ILS leaves the radios alone and shows nothing.
  • When it leaves them alone. It never retunes while the localizer is captured or while the ILS it tuned is being received.
  • The MCDU RAD NAV page. The page key RAD NAV is in the row of page keys (INIT, F-PLN, DIR, PROG, PERF, RAD NAV). It shows VOR1 (frequency on line key L1, course on R1) and the ILS: its frequency, its ident and its course, with AUTO beside them when the computer tuned it. To tune it yourself, type a frequency from 108.00 to 117.95 on the keypad and press L2. The page then says MANUAL. Your frequency stays until you change the arrival runway or the destination. To give the tuning back, type AUTO and press L2.

C172Flying an ILS in the Cessna

The Cessna has no autopilot and no flight director. You fly the two needles by hand. Plan for a calm, stable approach. The numbers below are the aircraft's own: stall 48 knots clean and 40 with full flaps, flaps at 10 degrees only below 110 knots and beyond that only below 85, and 65 to 75 knots with the flaps up or 60 to 70 with them down for landing. The landing lesson uses flaps 20 at about 65 knots and about 1,700 RPM.

  1. Choose the runway. Pick an end from the table. Note its frequency and its heading (the runway number times ten). Check the wind and the length.
  2. Tune and identify. Put the frequency on NAV2 and check the ident under the dial. The red NAV flag drops out once you are inside the beam (18 nm, within 35 degrees of the centreline). The red GS flag drops out within 10 nm and 8 degrees.
  3. Set the altimeter to the airport's QNH, which ATC gives you. For a CAT I approach, the decision height is the airport elevation plus 200 ft on the altimeter.
  4. Stay below the glide path. Fly level at 2,000 to 3,000 ft above the airport, toward the extended centreline, at an angle of about 30 degrees to it. The path is 1,900 ft above the airport at 6 nm. Slow to about 90 knots and take flaps 10 if you like.
  5. Join the localizer. When the vertical needle comes alive and moves toward the centre, start the turn to the runway heading at about two dots. Then fly the runway heading and correct small errors: if the needle drifts left, turn left 2 to 5 degrees, and come back to the runway heading when it centres. Turn toward the needle, in small steps. The needle is more sensitive close in.
  6. Wait for the glide slope. Within 10 nm the horizontal needle shows. It starts high, because the path is above you. Stay level until it reaches the centre. Slow to about 70 knots and take flaps 20 as you come to it, and trim.
  7. Go down the path. As the needle centres, reduce the power to about 1,700 RPM and lower the nose to hold 65 to 70 knots. That gives a descent of about 350 ft/min in still air. The rule is ground speed times 5.3 (table below). Needle above the centre: you are below the path, so reduce the descent. Needle below the centre: descend faster. Use power for the rate and small pitch changes for the speed. Keep the localizer needle centred with the heading.
  8. At the decision height look up. The approach lights, then the runway and the PAPI show up. If you see the runway and can land, continue. If not, go around. The sim does not call minimums for you.
  9. Land. Cross the threshold near 60 knots, close the throttle, flare and land on the main wheels. Brake with B. The beam leads to the touchdown zone, a few hundred metres past the threshold.

The airliner can land itself on an ILS. The autopilot flies the flare and the roll out on the runway, and the autothrust brings the thrust to idle.

What it needs. All four of these:

  • both autopilots, AP1 and AP2 (the FMA says AP1+2);
  • an ILS received with its glide slope, with the localizer and the glide slope captured (LOC and G/S, not LOC* and G/S*);
  • the gear down;
  • the flaps at CONF 3 or FULL.

The runway and its lights

A runway end with an ILS looks different from one without, by day and at night. The lights show at night, within 25 km of the airport. The paint shows all day.

  • Approach lights, ILS end. A line of white bars on the centreline, every 30 m, out to 900 m. Crossbars at 150 m and 300 m. Red side rows over the inner 270 m. A sequence of flashes runs toward the runway, from 900 m in to 330 m, twice a second.
  • Approach lights, other end. A runway of 1,200 m or more gets a simple system of 3-light bars every 60 m, out to 420 m, with one crossbar at 300 m. A shorter runway has none.
  • Touchdown zone. An ILS end has touchdown-zone light bars over its first 900 m and the paired touchdown-zone bars painted on the runway. An end without an ILS has no touchdown-zone bars: it is painted as a runway for a non-precision approach.
  • Centreline. Centreline lights run along any runway with an ILS end, and along runways of 2,000 m or more. On an instrument runway the last 600 m of edge lights are amber.
  • PAPI. Every runway end has a PAPI (precision approach path indicator) on the left: two white and two red lights on a 3 degree path, visible by day. After the decision height, use it to hold the same path as the ILS.

ATC and the route planner

  • A320On the airliner the phrases are IFR-style. You call request ILS approach runway 27. If that runway has no ILS in the sim, the call and the reply say visual instead. The controller answers with a descent, the pressure setting and the clearance: descend 3000 feet, QNH 1012, cleared ILS approach runway 27, contact Tower. The tower says continue approach runway 27, report established. You call established ILS runway 27, and then you hear cleared to land with the wind. After a go-around: climb straight ahead 3000 feet, expect vectors for another approach. The controller names the runway end you are flying toward. It gives no radar vectors and no speed instructions, and the altitudes are generic.
  • C172On the Cessna the flight is VFR. The controller gives a runway, the pressure setting and a way to join, then report final and cleared to land. It never says ILS. The ILS is for you to fly. Landing without a clearance draws a note, and it does not stop you. See ATC.
  • The route planner. On the Route step, the runway chips next to To pick the arrival runway: Any, or one end. The chips do not show which ends have an ILS, so use the table. A chosen end adds a fix 8 nm out on its final. On the airliner it also fills ARR RWY on the MCDU, and the flight computer tunes that runway's ILS once you are within 100 nm. Off unless you turn it on, on the Weather step or with --live-weather. Lessons ignore it.

Wind, cloud and rain

  • The surface wind strengthens with height, then blends toward the wind aloft. Gusts and lulls come and go around the wind you set, so 20G30 averages 20 kt and swings between about 13 and 30. They fade out above about 1000 m. The Western Ghats give ridge lift on the windward side, a few m/s in a 20 kt westerly and still felt a kilometre or more above the slope, and sink and turbulence on the lee side. In a strong wind across the escarpment, waves stand downwind of it: bands of smooth lift and sink of 1–5 m/s every 6–20 km, reaching well above the hills, with a rough rotor low down under the first one.
  • Turbulence follows the military standard most simulators use. The slider reads None, Light (about a third), Moderate (two thirds) and Severe (the end). Near the ground it is the gustiness of a 15, 30 or 45 kt wind; higher up it follows the standard's light, moderate and severe levels and no longer grows with the wind aloft. Near the ground the gusts along and across your path are longer and stronger than the up-and-down ones, and every bump up or down fades out over the last 10 m above the wheels, so the flare is smooth. Gusts also roll and yaw the aircraft a little; the Cessna's short wings feel it more than the airliner's long ones. Cumulus and storm cloud are rough even with the slider at None.
  • Thermals and storm cells are their own sliders. Thermals run about 1–3 m/s under cumulus by day, stronger in the middle of the layer, none over a cold sea or under a thick deck. The air between them sinks just as much as the thermals lift, so flying straight on you neither gain nor lose height on average. Storm cells, in monsoon cloud, can put a downdraft of about 10 m/s in the core and a shear near the ground, and beside the core an updraft of up to 18 m/s in the middle of the cloud. Monsoon storm clouds reach about 11 km; heavy showers from cumulus push down at up to 5 m/s in shafts about a kilometre across. A card says wind shear. A cell's wind fades out at its edge, with no jolt.
  • Inside cloud, cockpit visibility falls to about 100 m in the dense parts. Outside views keep the aircraft visible. Cloud never draws over the cockpit itself, and no card announces entering or leaving cloud: the view and the instruments show it. Rain falls only below the tops, beads on the windshield, and darkens the ground. Near the ground you see drops splash around you and rings open on wet runways, taxiways, aprons and puddles. On a wet runway the wheels throw spray, more as you speed up, with a tall plume behind the main wheels at takeoff and landing speeds. At full power standing still, the propeller blows a mist back over wet ground. Heavy rain lengthens a braked stop, to about 1.8 times the dry distance at full rain. Rain falls from the thick parts of the cloud and drifts with it; below a rain setting of 0.3 it falls only as showers under the biggest clouds. What rain does to the aircraft depends on where the aircraft is, whichever view you watch from.
  • QNH and temperature change the density altitude. The Kollsman window starts at the QNH you chose.

Icing

Ice forms in cloud between about +2 °C and −20 °C. On a 30 °C day in this region that band is roughly FL150 to FL250, so the airliner can ice there and the Cessna almost never does. Monsoon storm clouds reach through it. Their tall parts above the drawn cloud layer are felt but not drawn: you fly from clear air into cloud. The icing slider is how much supercooled water the cloud holds. Presets set it; Monsoon is the highest. Storm cells add their own share.

A full load of airframe ice adds drag and raises the stall speed. Inlet ice costs power or thrust. Pitot ice and static ice behave like the blocked-port failures until the ice falls off. Alternate static bypasses an iced static port.

C172Pitot heat, with the battery bus on, is the protection. There is no wing de-ice.

A320Wing and engine anti-ice run from engine bleed and cost a little thrust. Probes heat on their own. The warning display shows ICE DETECTED until anti-ice is on.

Sun, moon and night

The sun is the real sun for this map. The default flight is 10:00 IST on 14 February. F10 can run the clock faster. The moon has the phase and the place for the date, including a glitter path on the water. City glow sits on the horizon. Night exposure is lifted so the coast and the cloud decks stay readable.

Dawn and dusk follow the sun. In the half hour before sunrise the sky brightens from a dark blue, the last stars go out, and high cirrus only light up once the sun reaches them. A low sun is weak, so the first half hour after sunrise stays soft and dim, and full daylight comes as the sun climbs. Your eyes no longer adjust a sunrise to look like midday.

What you can hit

Touching a solid with any part of the airframe ends the flight, in the air or on the ground. The card names the part and the thing. Weight and balance

The flight model uses the weight and the centre of gravity you loaded. An aft centre of gravity lightens the pitch. The airliner chart is an approximate public shape, in percent of mean aerodynamic chord, and the page says so.

Trees follow the land. Coconut palms crowd the Konkan and Kerala villages and paddies, mango, banyan and peepal shade the towns and farms, teak and evergreen forest covers the Ghats, and thorny acacia scrub dots the dry plateau. Up close (within 90 m on Medium, 120 m on High, 150 m on Ultra, 250 m on Max) they are real trees with branches, leaves you can see the sky through, and dappled shadows. Coconut palms have slender ringed trunks that curve and lean, long feathery fronds, dead brown fronds hanging below and a bunch of coconuts; casuarinas hang wispy needles. Further out each tree is a picture taken from 64 directions, so a forest looks right from the side and from straight above, and is lit by the sun. High and up blend between the pictures as you fly past; Low and Medium use the nearest one, and Low draws no close-up trees at all. On High and up the distant trees cast real shadows too, out to 500 m (High), 800 m (Ultra) or 1200 m (Max). Beyond that, and above 1500 m over the ground, the ground under a forest is shaded and forests cast a soft shadow on open land away from the sun. The leaves follow the season: green after the monsoon, olive and brown on teak and scrub by May (the date of the flight, --day=N).

Near the ground, grass grows on meadows and farmland, ferns and bushes under the forest, and sparse dry grass on the plateau and the bare laterite. None grows on runways, taxiways, aprons, roads or water. It sways a little in the wind and dries with the season. You see it within 40 m on High, 50 m on Ultra and 65 m on Max, within 18 m on Medium, and not at all on Low; it fades out at the edge, and is gone once you are more than 120 m above the ground.

Edge smoothing (Options, Graphics) takes the jagged steps off wing, runway and building edges. Auto uses SMAA on Low, Medium and High: it keeps runway paint, windows and instrument text sharper than the older FXAA did. Ultra and Max smooth edges with TAA instead, and so does FSR2 when the picture is below 100 % on Forward+, so the setting does nothing there. Pick FXAA for the cheapest smoothing on a very weak graphics chip, or Off.

Ground effects follow the preset too: rain splashes, wheel spray, propeller wash and dust use more particles on higher presets. Low has none of them and keeps only the rain rings on wet pavement, which cost almost nothing. Medium has splashes, spray, wash and dust with fewer particles, and High and up add the plume behind the main wheels. None of it glows at night, and it fades out right in front of the camera so it never fills the cockpit or chase view.

Your aircraft always has a shadow on the ground. On Low, where the sun casts none, the game draws one from the aircraft's outline, thrown the way the sun is shining and fading with clouds, dusk and height. On Medium the sun's own shadow holds the aircraft up close, and the drawn one takes over when the camera is far back. Every preset also darkens the ground softly right under the belly, engines and wheels.

Motion blur (High and up) blurs the scenery only as much as the camera's own turn and movement explain: steady flight stays sharp, a fast pan or a hard turn streaks. Switch it off under Options, Graphics if you want every frame still-sharp.

Small things at an airport (service vehicles, stand numbers, lamp masts, signs) are drawn only while they are big enough on screen to see, a few pixels on a 1080p picture. Higher presets keep them further out, and zooming in (tower view, fly-by) brings them back. A distant jetway is drawn as one simple block and turns into the full bridge as you get closer; the bridge at your own aircraft is always drawn in full. Terminals, windsocks, runway and apron lights and lit windows stay at every distance. On the ground the game also skips what is hidden behind terminal buildings. On Low and Medium a far object can switch off at once instead of fading out; at that size it is only a few pixels.

Runways and taxiways are built only for airports near you, and dropped again when you fly away. How near depends on the size of the runway and on the preset: a long runway is still built far off, a small airstrip only close in. The airports within 15 km of the start are ready before the first frame. This cuts the number of draw calls and the memory the sim uses.

With Render resolution on Auto, the game measures how long the graphics card takes per frame. If it cannot keep up with your screen, the 3D picture is drawn a step smaller, down to 70 %, and brought back when there is room. Where the graphics driver does not report that time (on a Mac), the game uses the time per frame instead: when frames come late it steps down, and after 20 steady seconds it tries one step back up, waiting longer each time a step up does not hold. The same rule runs on every computer. F3 is the performance overlay. If a frame is late, drop a preset before editing the terrain node.

Read-only installsAppImage and Flatpak cannot save a renderer. Pass --rendering-method forward_plus on the command line.

The engine will not start

C172Shift+M, hold ' about two seconds, tap Page Up, M three times, hold S. The full order is in OPS 2. A dead battery means no starter.

A320Ctrl+I twice so the mode is IGN/START, then Ctrl+D and Ctrl+E, and wait about 45 seconds. The full order is in OPS 3.

The Cessna keeps rolling left

That is the solo weight and the engine. It is about neutral at 100 KIAS in level flight. In the climb, hold a little right aileron and rudder. See OPS 2.

ATC does not answer

COM1 has to be the frequency of the unit you called, and the avionics have to be powered. With radio assist on, the call tunes it. With assist off, pick Tune COM1 in the radio list. Fields with no published frequency answer on any frequency. A training lesson stays silent until you call.

Is the ILS real?

At the ten airports whose runways have an ILS in the AIP, yes: the ident, frequency and category are the published ones. The antennas and the beams are the sim's own, and the data is for the game only, not for navigation. At Shivamogga and Sindhudurg, whose long runways have none in the AIP, the ILS is simulated and the map marks it with a star. The list is in Which airports and runways have an ILS.

Can I fly online, or with today's weather?

There is no multiplayer and no live traffic. Live weather is optional, on the Weather step and with --live-weather. It sends a station id or a coarse position, and nothing else. See OPS 7.

Where are my files?

In the Godot user folder: last_flight.json, key and joystick bindings, options.cfg, audio.cfg, graphics.cfg, saves/ and liveries/. On Linux, ~/.local/share/godot/app_userdata/libresim/.

What is not in the sim yet?

A Cessna autopilot, spoken ATC with real radio sounds, other traffic in the circuit, and a fuller airliner: published SIDs and STARs, constraint altitudes, VNAV predictions, EXPED, the automatic changeover between knots and Mach, and the finer points of the autoland (one-autopilot landings, CAT 2, decision heights and the monitors). Instrument approaches other than the ILS (VOR, NDB and RNAV approaches) and published approach charts are not in the sim either. The route you can file today already follows published Indian airways. The airliner's autopilot limits are in OPS 3, and the autoland's in Autoland.
